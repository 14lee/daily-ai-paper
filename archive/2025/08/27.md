| Optimal Sparsity of Mixture-of-Experts Language Models for Reasoning

The primary objective was to determine how MoE sparsity influences memorization and reasoning capabilities, and whether this optimal sparsity changes with task type, post-training methods, or test-time compute. The methodology involved training families of Mixtral-style MoE Transformers with systematically varied total parameters, active parameters, and top-k routing under fixed compute budgets, then evaluating pre-training loss, downstream task loss, and accuracy on memorization (TriviaQA, HellaSwag) and reasoning (GSM8K, GSM-Plus) benchmarks. Primary results indicate that memorization benchmarks improve monotonically with total parameters, whereas reasoning performance for tasks like GSM8K and GSM-Plus saturates and can regress (task loss increases after training loss drops below a threshold), a trend robust to GRPO and increased test-time compute. This implies that identifying optimal MoE sparsity during pre-training is critical for reasoning models under fixed compute budgets, as excessive sparsity can degrade reasoning performance despite lower training loss, and this cannot be recovered post-training. |

| Unraveling the cognitive patterns of Large Language Models through
  module communities (Read more on [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18192) or [HuggingFace](https://huggingface.co/papers/2508.18192))| Jianxi Gao, Pin-Yu Chen, KBhandari11 | This paper introduces a network-based framework to analyze Large Language Models' (LLMs) cognitive patterns by mapping skills, datasets, and architectural modules. The primary objective is to understand LLM internal cognitive mechanisms by linking cognitive skills, LLM architectures, and datasets, drawing an analogy to biological brain organization. The authors construct a multipartite network of skills, datasets, and LLM modules, then project it into skill and module networks, applying Louvain community detection, spectral analysis, and a Taylor expansion-based pruning strategy to assess module importance for specific skills. LLMs (Llama, Llama-Chat, Vicuna) are then fine-tuned under community-specific, random, and all-module configurations. Primary results indicate that LLMs do not exhibit focalized specialization like some biological systems; Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) scores for skill communities do not improve under any pruning strategy (Figure 3e), and fine-tuning across all modules yields the highest overall accuracy (Figure 5f), distinguishing LLMs' distributed knowledge representation. The principal implication for AI practitioners is that effective fine-tuning strategies for LLMs should prioritize leveraging distributed learning dynamics and network-wide dependencies rather than rigid, localized modular interventions. |
